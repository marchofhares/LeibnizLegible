# Vision-language models (VLMs) and multimodal LLMs for historical HTR: 17th/18th-century German Kurrent and early-modern Latin/French cursive (evidence as of 26 Oct 2026)

Conventions: CER/CRR numbers are quoted as each source reports them; metrics, normalisation, unit (page vs line) and sample sizes differ and are flagged per item. "ESTIMATE" marks my own arithmetic or engineering estimates. "(derived)" marks simple arithmetic on a cited number. Vendor blogs, Substack posts, GitHub READMEs and Hugging Face cards are labelled as non-peer-reviewed. Full text was read for the arXiv papers behind the main numbers (2503.15195, 2410.24034, 2411.03340, 2504.00414, 2509.19768, 2512.19675, 2605.26712, 2609.20064, 2605.00977, 2605.30235, 2508.11499, 2602.14524, 2607.03836); 2507.04132, 2510.06743 and 2510.14528 were read in part; 2503.19546, 2509.09722, 2605.27750 and 2501.11623 only as abstracts.

## 1. Reading pages/lines directly (zero-shot): is "VLMs read Kurrent at 70-80 % CER" still true, and what do 2025-26 studies report for German, Latin and French cursive?

### Takeaway
The 70-80 % figure is right for 2024-vintage models on Bozen READ-2016 pages and still roughly right for self-hosted open 3-9B VLMs (which loop and hallucinate), but it is out of date for frontier APIs: independent 2026 page-level tests on early-modern German hands give about 35-60 % CER for Claude Opus 4.5 / Gemini 3 Pro on 10-page samples (Gemini 2.5 Pro about 26 % normalised edit distance on CHURRO's German handwriting), still several times worse than line-level CTC/TrOCR models (about 5-10 %), and the one 2026 Kurrent-specific study (DHd 2026, Goethe manuscripts) found MLLMs alone insufficient and sometimes strongly hallucinating. French (Gemini 3 Pro about 10 % CER on 1600-1800 manuscripts) and Latin pages (about 14-16 %) are much easier for the best API models, but I found no Kurrent-only or early-modern-Latin benchmark of the newest models (Gemini 3.1/4, Claude Opus 5.x, GPT-5.5/6). The project's own Phase B1 line-level test on Leibniz's Latin/French hand (150 lines) measured 36.6-78.7 % CER for GPT-4.1/4o, GPT-5.6 and Gemini 3.8 Flash against 8.2 % for the fine-tuned kraken model, but it did not include Claude or Gemini Pro-tier models, did not measure Kurrent, and the "70-80 % on Kurrent" line in the project's SPECS is literature-derived rather than measured.

### Cited Findings

**A. Where the 70-80 % figure comes from (2024-vintage models, older results)**
- Crosilla, Klic and Colavizza ran eight MLLMs (state of the art as of Oct 2024) zero-shot on full pages, same prompt, temperature 0. READ2016 (German, 15th-19th c., 50 pages, Bozen council minutes): CER Claude 3.5 Sonnet 71.17 %, Qwen2-VL-7B 76.37 %, Pixtral-12B 77.88 %, GPT-4o-mini 78.51 %, GPT-4o 80.20 %, MiniCPM-V 2.6 81.33 %, InternVL2-8B 81.62 %; Transkribus "Text Titan I" 40.63 % on the same pages. ICDAR2017 (German/French/Italian, 19th c., 500 pages): Claude 41.19 %, Qwen2-VL-7B 58 %, GPT-4o-mini 58.94 %, GPT-4o 60.98 % vs Text Titan I 14.40 %. English Bentham: Qwen2-VL-7B 8.01 % vs Titan 7.07 %; modern IAM 1.7-2.3 %. Metric: CER after normalising output length to the reference and stripping line breaks/punctuation. Published as J. Documentation 81(7) 2025. — [arXiv 2503.15195](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.15195), DOI cited in [METATR refs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.26712)
- Memorisation check (cloze test on benchmark transcriptions): GPT-4o 17/300 masked words, Claude 3.5 Sonnet 27/300, open models 0-2/300, so contamination of READ2016 looks small. — [arXiv 2503.15195](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.15195)
- Older still: a CHR-2024 paper (gemini-pro-vision) reported 74 % CER on 17th-century German vs 9.2 % for TrOCR fine-tuned on 500 annotated pages (53 % with 30 pages); it attributes the worst cases to hallucinated text rather than misread letters. — [arXiv 2410.24034](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.24034)

**B. Frontier API models on German handwriting, 2025-26**
- Transkribus (vendor-run; blog 20 Jun 2025; 2,000 held-out pages, 13 languages; refusals/empty outputs scored as 100 % CER; only the best LLM per category is reported): handwriting CER Text Titan I-ter 10.8 % vs Gemini 2.5 Flash 35.0 %; German 8.6 % vs Gemini 2.0 Flash 34.7 % (German Genius model 9.0 %); French 9.1 % vs Gemini 2.5 Pro 23.2 %; "other languages" (incl. Latin) 9.6 %, described as 3.4x better than the best LLM (about 33 %, derived). — [Transkribus blog](https://blog.transkribus.org/en/new-text-titan-i-ter-and-how-it-compares-to-chatgpt-gemini-and-other-llms)
- CHURRO-DS handwritten test split (30 test pages per language; CHURRO-DS holds 2,358 German, 1,596 Latin and 3,627 French handwritten pages in total; page level; metric is normalised Levenshtein similarity, so 1 - similarity approximates, but is not identical to, CER). German: Gemini 2.5 Pro 73.9, Gemini 2.5 Flash 69.4, Claude Sonnet 3.7 58.6, GPT-5 Mini 56.3, GPT-4.1 Mini 55.7, Claude Opus 4.1 52.3, Claude Sonnet 4 48.6, GPT-4o 39.1, GPT-5 35.4 (distance about 26, 31, 41, 44, 44, 48, 51, 61, 65 %, derived). Authors: the gap between Gemini and other proprietary VLMs is "considerably larger" on handwriting than print; best-of-all-systems oracle for German handwriting 89.4. The German handwritten pool includes ICFHR-2016 READ/Bozen, the Dresden Hofdiarium 1665 (17th-c. Kurrent, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) and Escher letters, but the Kurrent subset is not reported separately. — [CHURRO, EMNLP 2025, arXiv 2509.19768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19768)
- METATR (Teklia, 26 May 2026; 453 images from 17 datasets, mostly 10 images per dataset and 10 per language sampled from CHURRO; CER lower-cased with line breaks mapped to spaces, not capped at 100 %; default decoding; Gemini 3 Pro run with thinking). READ-2016 (German 1470-1805 administrative manuscripts) CER: Claude Opus 4.5 48.8 %, Gemini 3 Pro 56.3 %, GPT-5.1 72.8 %, Mistral OCR 72.3 %, Azure Layout 72.9 %, PeroOCR 84.7 %, Churro-3B 15.4 % (its training set CHURRO-DS includes the ICFHR-2016 READ corpus, so this is in-domain and overlap is not excluded). CHURRO-sourced German pages (print and handwriting mixed): Gemini 3 Pro 34.8 %, Claude Opus 4.5 59.5 %, GPT-5.1 69.6 %. All handwritten documents in the benchmark: Gemini 3 Pro 28.5 %, Claude Opus 4.5 31.2 %, Mistral OCR 43.6 %, Azure Layout 49.1 %, GPT-5.1 59.5 %. German aggregated over the three German sets (printed BnL newspapers, CHURRO German pages, READ-2016): Gemini 3 Pro 33.9 %, Claude Opus 4.5 53.6 %, Churro 51.4 %, PeroOCR 63.0 %, GPT-5.1 66.7 %, Qwen3-VL-8B 78.1 %, olmOCR-2 106.5 %. Authors conclude proprietary models are the most consistent and that the findings challenge the notion that ATR is a solved problem. (Column order of the PDF tables was verified from word coordinates.) — [METATR, arXiv 2605.26712](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26712)
- Contrast, English: Gemini 3 Pro-preview 1.67 % strict CER (0.69 % "modified") on 50 English 18th/19th-c. handwritten documents (10,000 words; temperature 0; thinking at minimum), Claude Opus 4.5 4.28 %, Claude Sonnet 3.7 10.45 %, an OpenAI model 16.81 %, Transkribus raw 8 % and about 3 % after training on dozens of pages; about 1 cent per page at $2/$12 per M tokens. The post reports no German or Latin test. Non-peer-reviewed. — [Generative History](https://generativehistory.substack.com/p/gemini-3-solves-handwriting-recognition)
- Anecdotal comments under that post: one reader tested Gemini 3 on historical German Gothic handwriting, Russian and Latvian and found results "surprisingly good" (no numbers; Kurrent/Sütterlin not named); another found a medieval Latin manuscript much harder (the model guessed names). — [comments](https://generativehistory.substack.com/p/gemini-3-solves-handwriting-recognition/comments)
- Vendor-run benchmark (Inksight; runs 6 and 14 Oct 2026; 31 configurations; 205 pages incl. ScaDS German; pages capped at 100 % error): best average normalised CER on a Latin-script handwriting selection (103 pages) Claude Sonnet 5.5 (low) 8.1 %, Claude Opus 5 8.2 %, Gemini 3.7 Flash 8.4 %, Claude Opus 5.5 8.4 %, Grok 4.5 8.6 %; no per-dataset numbers published and not historical Kurrent. — [Inksight](https://www.inksight-app.com/handwriting-ocr-benchmark)
- Directly on German Kurrent, DHd 2026 (Vienna; Brüning, Hofmann-Polster, Schenke, Goethe- und Schiller-Archiv, Klassik Stiftung Weimar; 25 handwritten pages each from "Briefe an Goethe" and "Goethes Lyrik", 18th/19th-c. German Kurrent plus Latin-script hands, some French; each page run five times per model; CER without any normalisation; models named in the paper: GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 2.5 Pro; the repository README lists Claude Sonnet 4.5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Gemini 3 Pro Preview and GPT-5.2): MLLMs alone gave "deutlich schlechtere" (clearly worse) recognition than Transkribus; Gemini 2.5 Pro was better in individual cases but the spread was so large that MLLMs alone are insufficient; the paper reports "stark halluzinierte Ergebnisse" (strongly hallucinated results) for the letters subcorpus and says generative models cannot yet compete with specialised HTR outside English and Latin-script hands. The CER table itself (Table 1) is not in the published PDF or the repository README, so no numbers could be retrieved. — [DHd 2026 paper (Zenodo, CC BY 4.0)](https://zenodo.org/records/18696394), [workflow repo](https://github.com/KlassikStiftungWeimar/atr-workflow-gsa-public)
- A blog claim that frontier VLMs "collapse to 70-98 % CER on 18th-century German archival material" names no models, data or method (unsourced; do not rely). — [okraPDF](https://okrapdf.com/blog/handwriting-ocr-benchmark/)
- Few-shot and small-scale zero-shot studies (abstract-level only): a DiTTET 2025 study of five VLMs on contemporary English and early-modern Spanish (RODRIGO) reports that proprietary models "approach the reliability of dedicated recognisers" while compact open models are competitive and could improve with light adaptation (numbers paywalled, as summarised in a search snippet) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-99474-6_24); Belgian probation-record tables (handwritten, line-by-line vs whole scan): two-shot GPT-4o (lines) and two-shot Claude 3.5 Sonnet (whole scans) gave transcriptions closest to ground truth and beat EasyOCR/Tesseract/TrOCR on CER and BLEU, with human evaluation disagreeing with CER/BLEU on whole scans (no numbers in the abstract) — [arXiv 2501.11623](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11623)
- English print/hand benchmark for historians (July 2026, English only): on 50 early-19th-c. Lower Canada administrative hands Gemini 3.5 Flash CER 1.52 %, Infinity Parser 2 2.72 %, Chandra 2 3.89 %, olmOCR 2 5.34 %, GLM-OCR 7.98 %; no German/Latin. — [Reading the Archive by Machine](https://working-papers-in-critical-search.github.io/paper-004-ocr-benchmark/)

**F. Failure modes reported for direct reading**
- Runaway repetition, hallucination and reading-order errors in open models (above); Gemini API `RECITATION` refusals on 3 of 100 early-modern print pages and a `MAX_TOKENS` truncation (Reading the Archive); recitation flags about 1 in 30 documents (Humphries 2024). — [Reading the Archive](https://working-papers-in-critical-search.github.io/paper-004-ocr-benchmark/), [arXiv 2411.03340](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.03340)
- Silent orthographic regularisation: a fine-tuned Qwen2.5-VL-7B had lower CER than TrOCR-large on 18th-c. printed English lines (0.60 % vs 1.05 %) but showed "selective linguistic regularization and orthographic normalization"; in the English benchmark most olmOCR/Infinity hallucinations were modernisations (e.g. "bloud" to "blood"). — [arXiv 2602.14524](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14524), [Reading the Archive](https://working-papers-in-critical-search.github.io/paper-004-ocr-benchmark/)
- VLM errors stay fluent: on Ancient Greek editions VLMs produce plausible substitutions where OCR engines give local noise, and decode-time interventions did not restore grounding. — [arXiv 2605.27750](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27750)
- Giving a VLM line boxes from a conventional OCR engine did not help in CHURRO's test: "Azure OCR + Gemini 2.5 Pro" scored 47.1 on German handwriting vs 73.9 for Gemini alone and 49.4 for Azure alone (box quality may be the cause). — [arXiv 2509.19768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19768)

- Named benchmarks that do evaluate VLMs on historical pages: [CHURRO-DS (EMNLP 2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19768), [METATR (Teklia, 2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26712), [Reading the Archive by Machine (English)](https://working-papers-in-critical-search.github.io/paper-004-ocr-benchmark/), [Inksight (vendor)](https://www.inksight-app.com/handwriting-ocr-benchmark). No "HTR benchmark for VLMs" with a dedicated Kurrent split was found.

**H. The project's own evidence (Leibniz Legible) and the source of the 70-80 % premise**
- The project SPECS (section 1.6) state: "No Leibniz German/Kurrent GT anywhere; frontier LLMs are catastrophic on Kurrent (70-80% CER zero-shot)", with no citation or Kurrent measurement; the number matches the 2024-vintage READ2016 results (section A). — [project SPECS.md](https://github.com/marchofhares/leibnizlegible/blob/main/SPECS.md)
- Phase B1 report (E. T. Atlas, 16 Sep 2026, CC BY 4.0): on a seeded random 150-line subsample of the PHILIUMM Leibniz validation split (pre-segmented, dewarped single-line crops; Latin and French; Leibniz's secretary hand; micro-averaged CER, NFD plus whitespace-collapse normalisation, case and diacritics kept) the fine-tuned kraken model scores 8.19 % CER (95 % CI 6.55-9.97; 26.86 % WER) and zero-shot VLMs score: gpt-4.1 36.60 % (CI 32.93-40.25), gpt-4o 43.82 % (39.24-48.43), gemini-3.8-flash 55.11 % (37.54-76.71), gpt-5.6-sol 74.17 % (66.89-80.66), gpt-5.6-luna 76.24 % (69.89-81.48), gpt-5.6-terra 78.66 % (73.02-84.06), gpt-4.1-mini 167.88 % (31.18-404.29, runaway outputs); WER for the well-behaved models 70.7-91.1 %. The report summarises the "well-behaved" range as 36.6-78.7 %, "four to ten times worse" than the 7.95 % CER the fine-tuned model achieves on all 1,878 validation lines, and notes the larger models most often modernised archaic spelling. It states German and Kurrent are unmeasured. — [Leibniz Legible Phase B1 report](https://zenodo.org/records/22782817)
- The project's STATUS log records an earlier run of the same harness on the same 150 lines with a three-model panel (gpt-4o 45.87 %, gpt-4.1 38.62 %, gpt-4.1-mini 34.79 %), noting the smallest model scored best and flagging a prompt-engineering lever (modernisation) not pursued; the Anthropic adapter was built but no Claude comparison had been run. — [project STATUS.md](https://github.com/marchofhares/leibnizlegible/blob/main/STATUS.md)
- Not tested in B1: any Claude model, Gemini 3 Pro / 3.1 Pro / 4 Argon, Qwen/open VLMs, and any Kurrent lines; prompts and decoding settings are not given in the published PDF. The independent benchmarks above rank Gemini 3 Pro and Claude Opus 4.5 first on handwriting and OpenAI models behind them (METATR GPT-5.1 59.5 % vs Gemini 3 Pro 28.5 % on handwritten pages; CHURRO GPT-5 35.4 vs Gemini 2.5 Pro 73.9 German similarity), which is consistent with B1's GPT-heavy panel scoring worst. — [Leibniz Legible Phase B1 report](https://zenodo.org/records/22782817), [METATR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26712), [CHURRO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19768)

### Inferences
- The project's 70-80 % premise is not contradicted but is mis-scoped: it describes (i) 2024-vintage models on READ2016 pages and (ii) OpenAI's reasoning-class GPT-5.6 models on Leibniz Latin/French lines (74-79 %), while the strongest handwriting readers in the independent record (Gemini 3 Pro, Claude Opus 4.5+) were not run on the project's data and score roughly 2x better than GPT-class models on handwritten pages elsewhere; B1's best zero-shot result (gpt-4.1, 36.6 %) is still 4.5x worse than the fine-tuned model, so the "do not use zero-shot VLMs as the primary reader" decision stands, while "zero-shot VLMs are useless" is not shown.
- The premise needs refining rather than reversing: as a primary reader of 17th/18th-c. Kurrent, even the best zero-shot API VLMs in the independent record (about 35-60 % page-level CER on tiny samples) remain several times worse than line-level CTC/TrOCR models (5-10 %), so ruling zero-shot VLMs out as the primary reader still holds; "70-80 %" is no longer a fair number for Gemini 3-class or Claude Opus 4.5-class models, but is still fair for GPT-5-class and for open 3-9B models.
- Page-level CER mixes reading ability with segmentation, reading order and loops: even the specialist Text Titan I scored 40.6 % on READ2016 pages in the same protocol, so these page-level numbers overstate the pure character-reading gap; no line-level zero-shot VLM measurement on Kurrent was found, so question (a) "lines directly" is genuinely open.
- The French and Latin picture is better than the German one for the top API models (10-16 % CER range on small samples) but those samples are tiny (10 pages), include print, and do not include early-modern scholarly Latin cursive specifically; treat as indicative only.
- Different model generations improved quickly (Claude 3.5 Sonnet 71 % on READ2016 in 2024 vs Claude Opus 4.5 49 % in 2026 on a different 10-page sample; Gemini Pro Vision 74 % vs Gemini 3 Pro 56 %), so Gemini 3.1/4 and Claude Opus 5.x may be better than anything measured here, but that is extrapolation and unmeasured.

### Gaps
- No independent CER for Gemini 3.1 Pro, Gemini 4, Claude Opus 5/5.5, Claude Fable 5.x or GPT-5.5/6 on 17th/18th-c. Kurrent or on early-modern scholarly Latin/French cursive (Inksight Oct 2026 is vendor-run, aggregate, and not Kurrent).
- No line-level zero-shot VLM evaluation on Kurrent; no per-script (Kurrent-only) breakdown in CHURRO or METATR; METATR and CHURRO German handwriting samples are 10 and 30 pages respectively.
- No 2025-26 historical-handwriting evaluation found for Llama 3.2/4 Vision, Florence-2, Idefics3/SmolVLM, Kosmos-2.5, GOT-OCR, Nougat, PaddleOCR-VL, Gemma 3/4, InternVL beyond CHURRO's InternVL3.5-30B and Gemma 3 27B rows.
- No Bullinger, early-modern scholarly Latin (humanist hand) or Kurrent evaluation of any VLM; the ICDAR 2026 CMMHWR report was inaccessible (HAL bot-block), so the official ranking is unverified.
- The project's B1 VLM panel is 150 lines, GPT-heavy, with a single unpublished prompt, no Claude, no Gemini Pro-tier model and no Kurrent lines; a like-for-like test of Gemini 3.1 Pro/4 Argon and Claude Opus 5.5/Sonnet 5.5 on the same 150 lines (and on Kurrent lines such as the Dresden Hofdiarium set) would close the gap; no such run exists in the sources found.
- The numeric CER table of the DHd 2026 Weimar Kurrent evaluation (Table 1) is not in the PDF or README; contacting the authors or reading the repository's data folders (not listable here) would be needed.
- The Díez García et al. (2025, DiTTET) zero-shot study of five VLMs on English and early-modern Spanish (RODRIGO) was paywalled; its numbers were not retrieved.

## 2. Do fine-tuned open VLMs (LoRA on Qwen2.5-VL 3B/7B, Florence-2, etc.) beat TrOCR/kraken on small historical datasets, and at what compute (24 GB feasibility)?

### Takeaway
I found no published head-to-head in which a LoRA-tuned Qwen2.5-VL 3B/7B (or Florence-2) beats a kraken/TrOCR fine-tune on a few thousand lines of historical handwriting; VLM wins are reported only at 10^5-10^6 lines/pages (CHURRO, MEDUSA, Helsinki printed lines) and the strongest claim was not reproduced independently (Kiessling 2026), while CRNN/TrOCR baselines reach about 2-9 % CER with 4k-500k lines. LoRA/QLoRA is technically feasible on a 24 GB card for 3-4B models (bf16) and 7-9B models (4-bit), but the small-scale VLM LoRA results available have no baselines.

### Cited Findings

**A. Large-data fine-tunes (where fine-tuned VLMs do win)**
- CHURRO: Qwen2.5-VL-3B fully fine-tuned (not LoRA) on CHURRO-DS (99,491 pages, 155 corpora, 22 centuries, 46 language clusters), 5 epochs on 32 H100 GPUs, about 25 h (about 800 H100-hours, derived), LR 5e-5 cosine, effective batch 128, images capped at 5,120 patches of 28x28 px; all experiments in the paper used about 6,000 H100-hours. Gains over the base 3B on handwriting: German +31.9 points (51.2 to 83.1), Latin +19.2 (51.7 to 70.9), French +21.9 (59.2 to 81.1), average +27.2; it also beats Gemini 2.5 Pro by 6.5 points on handwriting and is stated to be 15.5x cheaper (APIs at 50 % batch discount). Weights are under the Qwen Research License (non-commercial); the dataset is released CC BY-SA 4.0 although some component corpora are CC BY-NC(-SA/-ND). — [arXiv 2509.19768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19768), [HF churro-3B](https://huggingface.co/stanford-oval/churro-3B)
- MEDUSA 0.1 / 0.2 (École nationale des chartes-PSL, May 2026): line-level Qwen3.5-4B and 9B recognisers trained on about 645k lines (Gold 423,092 + Platinum 221,987; v0.2 adds 500k synthetic lines), CC-BY-4.0 weights. ICDAR 2026 CMMHWR (as stated on the card): 4B official CER 8.15 / 5.60 / 12.0 % on tasks 1/2/3; unweighted internal CER 14.7 % (4B) and 13.2 % (9B, "best model submitted") vs 17.3 % for the kraken-CATMuS 1.6.0 baseline; inference batch 8, max 128 new tokens; trained on HPC (IDRIS/GENCI allocation). — [HF Medusa0.1Line-4B](https://huggingface.co/ENC-PSL/Medusa0.1Line-4B), [HF Medusa0.2Line-9B](https://huggingface.co/ENC-PSL/Medusa0.2Line-9B)
- Independent re-test: Kiessling (kraken's author, 17 Sep 2026) could not reproduce Medusa's reported task-1/2/3 CERs (8.03/5.24/10.8 %) with the released pipeline. His measurements (CER %, CMMHWR task 1 French / Latin / Spanish, task 2 Occitan, task 3 Czech, CoMMA Latin / French): kraken CRNN trained on CATMuS 5.01 / 12.61 / 10.47 / 8.38 / 19.84 / 13.48 / 21.65; PP-OCRv6-medium fine-tuned on CATMuS 3.36 / 8.70 / 7.99 / 6.62 / 18.05 / 11.30 / 21.36; Medusa (Qwen3.5-9B) 3.74 / 13.07 / 11.19 / 5.70 / 15.73 / 16.08 / 28.24. So against the kraken CRNN the VLM won on French (3.74 vs 5.01), Occitan and Czech but lost on Latin, Spanish and both CoMMA sets; against PP-OCRv6-medium it won only on Occitan and Czech (languages it had been trained on, which PP-OCRv6's pretraining lacked), and PP-OCRv6-medium was "approximately 45 times faster". — [Kiessling, arXiv 2609.20064](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20064)
- Fine-tuned compact VLM for German handwriting (HF card, non-peer-reviewed): LightOnOCR-3-1B fine-tuned on 21,038 pages (17.7 M characters, 12 subsets, mainly 1820-1880, Escher letters 62.5 %, Latin under 1 %); only vision encoder and projector trained (409.6 M of 1,005.6 M parameters), 10 h 56 min on one RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell (about 11 GPU-hours); CER 4.74 % vs 45.0 % for the stock model on 610 validation pages; hard hands remain poor (Humboldt late hand 28.4 %, Nägeli letters 13.2 %); 2 of 610 pages looped; weights CC BY-SA 4.0. — [HF card](https://huggingface.co/b-straub/LightOnOCR-3-1B-Historic-German-Handwriting)
- Printed 18th-c. English lines, about 195k training lines (62k manual, 68k silver, 65k synthetic): Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct fine-tuned with parameter-efficient tuning (vision encoder frozen) CER 0.60 % / WER 2.48 % vs TrOCR-large 1.05 % / 3.89 %, but with silent orthographic regularisation. — [arXiv 2602.14524](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14524)

**B. Small-data regime: what CRNN/TrOCR achieve**
- 4,029 lines of abbreviated medieval Latin: CNN+LSTM (CTC) 6.5 % CER / 20.6 % WER, with KenLM 6.0 / 17.8, TrOCR 6.9 / 16.3, versus 47 % CER for an off-the-shelf CATMuS model. — [arXiv 2605.00977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00977)
- Gwalther 16th-c. Latin: 3,603 training lines, TrOCR-base 20 epochs on an A100 40 GB, CER 1.86 % (single writer, random line split). — [arXiv 2508.11499](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11499)
- German, 17th c.: TrOCR fine-tuned on 30 annotated pages 53 % CER, on 500 pages 9.2 %; CNN-BiLSTM 42.1 % and 10.1 %. — [arXiv 2410.24034](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.24034)
- Few-shot adaptation: a kraken CRNN improved on all four CMMHWR manuscripts with only 20 training lines; PP-OCRv6 needed 40-80 lines; kraken CRNN runs at about 640 lines/s (A40, batch 32). — [arXiv 2609.20064](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20064)
- Autoregressive transformer HTR: fine-tuning "can reliably start with just 16 lines, yielding a 10 % relative improvement in CER" and about 40 % with 256 lines. — [arXiv 2503.19546](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19546)
- Engine comparison (Dutch Republic resolutions 1576-1795, handwriting; CER): HTR+ 1.78 %, IDA 3.54 %, Titan 3.61 %, PyLaia 3.89 %, TrOCR-f 3.97 %; TrOCR-f fine-tuning on "a few thousand lines" is quick, and about 10 h on about 40,000 lines on a V100. — [PMC12202554](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12202554/)
- At 500k lines (BullingerDB) TrOCR-large (9.1 %) and PyLaia (9.2 %) are essentially tied, so extra model scale buys little on that data. — [arXiv 2605.30235](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30235)
- Kiessling's framing: large VLMs have seen "limited practical uptake in historical automatic text recognition because of their computational cost, dependence on large-scale pretraining, and hallucination"; PP-OCRv6 (CTC) fine-tuned beat the 9B VLM line recogniser on Latin tasks. — [arXiv 2609.20064](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20064)

**C. Small-scale LoRA results on VLMs (non-peer-reviewed GitHub/HF write-ups)**
- AraManuscript-VL: Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct, LoRA r=128 / alpha=256 / dropout 0.05, LR 1e-5, batch 1 with accumulation 16, 2 epochs, BF16, twelve Arabic datasets; CER 6.63 %, WER 22.37 %; no baseline; training size and hardware not stated. — [GitHub](https://github.com/DSARU-CERIST/AraManuscript-VL)
- GSoC 2026 early-modern Spanish: Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct LoRA r=16 / alpha=32, LR 1e-5, 10 epochs, batch 1 with accumulation 2, bf16, A40/A100 cluster; per-image CER RODRIGO median 0.107 (mean 0.136), Orinoco mean 0.908 (median 0.282), Tridis mean 0.550 (median 0.566); no zero-shot or TrOCR/kraken comparison. — [GitHub](https://github.com/junghare-aniket/vlm-handwriting-recognition-es)
- Florence-2 for HTR: the searches in this task found no 2025-26 historical-handwriting fine-tuning results (model is MIT, 0.78 B parameters).

**D. 24 GB feasibility facts**
- bf16 weight sizes from Hugging Face safetensors metadata: Qwen2.5-VL-3B 3.75 B params (about 7.5 GB); Qwen2.5-VL-7B and olmOCR-2 8.29 B (about 16.6 GB); Qwen3-VL-4B 4.44 B; Qwen3-VL-8B 8.77 B (about 17.5 GB); Qwen3.5-4B 4.66 B; Qwen3.5-9B 9.65 B (about 19.3 GB); Medusa 4B 4.54 B and 9B 9.41 B; Gemma-4-12B 11.96 B (about 23.9 GB); LightOnOCR-2 1.01 B; PaddleOCR-VL 0.96 B; Florence-2-large 0.78 B. — [HF Qwen2.5-VL-7B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct), [HF Qwen3-VL-8B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct), [HF Qwen3.5-9B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B), [HF Medusa 9B](https://huggingface.co/ENC-PSL/Medusa0.1Line-9B), [HF gemma-4-12B-it](https://huggingface.co/google/gemma-4-12B-it)
- Ludwig's reference recipe fine-tunes Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct with 4-bit QLoRA (nf4, bf16 compute, batch 4, accumulation 8) "so it fits on a single 24 GB GPU". — [Ludwig](https://ludwig.ai/latest/examples/vlm_finetuning/)
- Unsloth advises image sizes of 300-1000 px and LoRA r=16 and lists a Qwen2.5-VL-7B handwriting-to-LaTeX notebook (no accuracy numbers given). — [Unsloth docs](https://unsloth.ai/docs/basics/vision-fine-tuning)
- Measured inference memory (A100, HF Transformers + FlashAttention-2, batch 1, peak): Qwen3-VL-8B 20.18 GiB, olmOCR-2 19.85, RolmOCR 20.62, Churro-3B 8.12, DeepSeek-OCR 7.57, LightOnOCR-2-1B 2.68. — [arXiv 2605.26712](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26712)
- LightOn states fine-tuning is "straightforward with common HF workflows" with LoRA/PEFT supported and base checkpoints intended for fine-tuning. — [HF blog](https://huggingface.co/blog/lightonai/lightonocr-2)

### Inferences
- At the "few thousand line pairs" scale the evidence favours the CTC/TrOCR route (known 2-9 % CER, tens of lines to adapt, hundreds of lines/s); VLM fine-tunes have shown clear gains only with 10^5+ pages/lines and not always against a well-tuned CRNN, so a LoRA-VLM experiment should be treated as a research bet, not an expected win.
- Realistic 24 GB plans (ESTIMATE from parameter counts plus the Ludwig recipe): Qwen2.5-VL-3B or Qwen3-VL-4B with bf16 LoRA on line crops fits comfortably; 7-9B models (Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Qwen3-VL-8B, Qwen3.5-9B, Medusa-9B) need 4-bit QLoRA or very short sequences with gradient checkpointing because bf16 weights alone take 16.6-19.3 GB; a few thousand line images for a few epochs would take on the order of hours, not days (rough ESTIMATE; no measured 4090 figure found).
- If a VLM line recogniser is tried, the most reusable starting points are Apache-2.0 bases (Qwen3-VL-4B/8B, Qwen3.5-4B/9B) or the CC-BY-4.0 Medusa weights (trained on medieval Latin-script lines, so transfer to early-modern Kurrent is untested); Qwen2.5-VL-3B-derived weights (e.g., CHURRO) are non-commercial.
- Page-level vs line-level matters: the strongest German result (LightOnOCR-3 fine-tune, 4.74 % CER) is page level but needed about 21k pages (several hundred thousand lines, ESTIMATE at about 30 lines per page) and mostly 19th-c. hands.

### Gaps
- No same-split comparison of LoRA-tuned Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B (or Qwen3-VL) vs kraken/TrOCR on a few thousand lines of Kurrent or early-modern Latin/French; no Florence-2 HTR results; no measured RTX 4090/3090 training-time or VRAM figures for HTR fine-tunes.
- The GitHub/HF fine-tune write-ups are single-author, without baselines, and some metadata (training size, hardware) is missing; the LightOnOCR-3 validation split may share collections/writers with training.
- Kiessling's Medusa numbers come from one author's reproduction using the released DocWorkflow pipeline; the competition organisers' official table was not accessible.

## 3. Using a VLM as a second reader alongside a CTC model (disagreement, consensus, combination)

### Takeaway
The closest published match to a "second reader" on German Kurrent is the Weimar (DHd 2026) workflow that merges a Transkribus transcription with an independent MLLM transcription in a third MLLM step under human control (claimed useful, but MLLMs alone insufficient; numbers not retrievable); the best-quantified second-reader practice is multi-model disagreement flagging with human adjudication on English handwriting (about 7 flagged differences per page, 4 % of text reviewed, strict CER 1.67 % to 1.25 % and modified 0.69 % to 0.23 %); I found no published study that combines a CTC recogniser's output with a VLM transcription on Kurrent or early-modern Latin, only analogues (CTC + LLM correction, HTR draft + multimodal correction) and evidence that CTC and VLM errors are complementary.

### Cited Findings
- German Kurrent, Goethe-era manuscripts (DHd 2026, Klassik Stiftung Weimar): a four-step workflow (1) the MLLM transcribes from the image alone, forcing independent image analysis; (2) the Transkribus plain-text output and the MLLM transcription are given to the MLLM with an instruction to compare and "merge" them into a version as faithful as possible to the handwriting; (3) tool-supported human comparison against the Transkribus output via a synoptic diff view; (4) TEI markup. Earlier single-step prompts (image plus HTR text to correct) failed because of "instruction conflict" (correct errors vs preserve spellings) and "instruction overload": the model normalised or modernised against the graphic evidence, and an explicit instruction to preserve historical spelling left senseless HTR errors uncorrected. The merged version mostly contained correct corrections (the authors' wording, translated); Claude Sonnet and Gemini outperformed GPT in the combination, and for the more legible Goethe poetry manuscripts the merge clearly improved on Transkribus CER, while for the letters (tables, French passages, unpublished texts) improvements over Transkribus were only partial; MLLMs alone were insufficient. The authors still require human control because LLM-introduced errors are masked by plausibility. — [DHd 2026 paper](https://zenodo.org/records/18696394)
- Disagreement flagging (Humphries and Leddy; non-peer-reviewed): baseline Gemini 3 Pro compared with Claude Opus 4.7 and Gemini 3.5 Flash on 10,060 words of English 18th/19th-c. records; 374 flagged differences (about 7 per page), 106 of them genuine errors (about 76 % of remaining errors); human review of about 4 % of the text; strict CER 1.67 % to 1.25 % and WER 4.42 % to 3.53 %; modified CER 0.69 % to 0.23 % and WER 1.33 % to 0.33 %. Premise: models from different families "tend to make different mistakes". — [Generative History](https://generativehistory.substack.com/p/when-models-disagree-transcription)
- Informal replication (not peer-reviewed; Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 5.5 on a 33-page Bentham stand-in): flags caught 59 % of the primary reader's errors while covering 10.4 % of words, but an automated model "resolver" (1.63 % CER) did not beat the primary reader (1.33 %), i.e. adjudication needed a human. — [GitHub issue 6547](https://github.com/Embassy-of-the-Free-Mind/sourcelibrary-v2/issues/6547)
- Heterogeneity matters: in the 2024 study, correction by a different model family improved strict CER by 15-20 % while self-correction changed scores by about 1 % (GPT-4o slightly worse); the authors argue LLM errors and HTR errors have different statistical properties (PyLaia errors are character-level such as "furr5", which LLMs fix easily). — [arXiv 2411.03340](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.03340)
- Consensus over augmented images: transcribing each image in several augmented variants (padding, blur, grid warp) with Gemini 2.0 Flash and fusing by alignment improved accuracy by 4 percentage points over a single shot on 622 Pennsylvania death records; grid-warp variants best separated high- from low-confidence cases (no CER reported). — [arXiv 2509.09722](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.09722)
- Run-to-run variance as an uncertainty signal: a Latin court-hand workflow ran Claude 3.7 Sonnet twice per 10-line chunk over the HTR text and image and flagged lines where the two runs differed by more than 5 % CER or both differed from the HTR text; flagged lines were excluded when training a new PyLaia model (5.25 % validation CER). — [arXiv 2507.04132](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.04132)
- Complementary error types: CTC errors are visually odd (the authors say an incomprehensible word "cues the user to return to the manuscript"); Gemini-corrected output had fewer errors but 3 of its 4 remaining errors in the examined excerpt were "misleading" (semantically plausible), and the CNN+LSTM transcriptions were easier to correct than TrOCR's. A fine-tuned Qwen VLM regularised spelling while TrOCR produced cascading errors. — [arXiv 2605.00977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00977), [arXiv 2602.14524](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14524)
- Page-level upper bound for choosing among systems: best-of-all-systems oracle on CHURRO German handwriting 89.4 vs 83.1 for the best single model and 73.9 for Gemini 2.5 Pro. — [arXiv 2509.19768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19768)

### Inferences
- The evidence supports the "different families, human arbitration" pattern; a CTC model is a natural member of such a pair because its errors are visually grounded and a VLM's are language-prior driven. Three untested designs follow (my inference): VLM as flagger (line-level alignment of CTC vs VLM text, send only disagreements to a human), VLM as corrector given image plus CTC draft with numbered line anchors, and VLM as scorer of CTC N-best lists; the third was not found in the literature.
- Review burden estimates from the English study (about 4-10 % of words flagged to catch 59-76 % of residual errors) are not transferable to Kurrent, where VLM zero-shot error rates are several times higher and disagreements would be far more frequent (ESTIMATE: likely tens of percent of lines flagged).

### Gaps
- The only Kurrent-specific second-reader study found (DHd 2026) has no retrievable CER table, models or settings are partly unclear (the paper text names GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4 and Gemini 2.5 Pro; the repository lists newer models), and it merges at page/text level with a general-purpose chat-style prompt rather than line-level alignment.
- No published CTC+VLM combination (ROVER, lattice/N-best rescoring, line-level voting) on historical handwriting; no Kurrent/Latin/French disagreement study; the English replication used a different corpus and is informal.

## 4. VLM post-correction of HTR output: which models and prompts help, which hallucinate (German/Latin evidence)

### Takeaway
Image-grounded correction by a different strong model helps (English handwriting: Transkribus Titan strict CER 8.0 % to 3.0 %; German Fraktur print: 3.67 % to 0.84 %; text-only Gemini 3 Pro on medieval Latin CTC output: CER 6.0 % to 3.8 %), but self-correction does not work, open-weight and weaker models often degrade the text, and corrected output contains more plausible-but-wrong errors; I found no controlled study on 17th/18th-c. Kurrent.

### Cited Findings
- Self-correction fails on German handwriting: three-step prompts ("Review the original image and your previous transcription ...") changed READ2016 CER by under 2 points for API models (Claude 3.5 Sonnet 71.17 % to 71.08 %, GPT-4o 80.20 % to 77.42 %), open models did not improve and often worsened (e.g., Qwen2-VL-7B IAM 2.30 % to 5.06 %); the authors' best case was GPT-4o on ICDAR2017 (about 8 points) but still unusable. The paper's post-correction tables XV-XVII look mislabelled or duplicated (the block labelled Bentham carries ICDAR2017-like values and Table XVII repeats Table XVI), so check dataset attribution before quoting those rows; the READ2016 figures above are from Table XVI. — [arXiv 2503.15195](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.15195)
- German Kurrent prompting lessons (DHd 2026, Weimar): single-step prompts asking the model to correct the HTR text using the image led to normalising miscorrections and plausible-looking misreadings replacing easily spotted HTR errors; adding an explicit instruction to preserve historical spelling and punctuation made the model leave nonsense HTR output unchanged (instruction conflict/overload); the workable pattern was a separate image-only MLLM transcription first, then a merge prompt comparing it with the HTR text, then human review against a diff. The paper cites a 2024 study (Boros et al.) finding that text-only LLM post-correction can even worsen historical transcriptions. — [DHd 2026 paper](https://zenodo.org/records/18696394)
- English handwriting, correction of Transkribus output with the page image (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini 1.5 Pro, GPT-4o; 50 pages): Titan strict CER 8.0 % to 3.0 % (Claude), 4.1 % (Gemini), 4.2 % (GPT-4o); PyLaia 10.3 % to 4.0 % / 5.4 % / 5.9 %; modified CER down to 1.8 %; cost about $0.005-0.01 per page for the correction call; LLM-on-LLM correction with a different model improved strict CER by 15-20 % while same-model correction by about 1 %. Prompt: use the handwritten page image to correct the draft, answer "Corrected Transcript:" followed by the text. — [arXiv 2411.03340](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.03340)
- German print (city directories 1754-1870, mostly Fraktur; normalised CER, Apr 2025): Gemini 2.0 Flash zero-shot 1.27 %, GPT-4o 6.31 %, Transkribus Text Titan I 4.16 %, Print M1 3.67 %; Print M1 draft corrected by Gemini 2.0 Flash with the image 0.84 % (0.08 % best directory), by GPT-4o 1.00 %. — [arXiv 2504.00414](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.00414)
- Latin, text-only correction of a CTC/TrOCR draft (no image sent), 4,029-line training set, 20 test cases: CNN+LSTM+KenLM 6.0 % CER / 17.8 % WER to 3.8 % / 11.9 % with Gemini 3 Pro; TrOCR 6.9 % / 16.3 % to 5.9 % / 14.9 %; per-case WER improved in 19 of 20 cases (worse in KB27-344Rm14, 14 % to 19 %). In one out-of-period excerpt (161 words) word accuracy rose from 85 % (24 errors) to 94 % (10 errors), but in the first four lines the raw CTC output had 10 errors (9 visibly wrong or fixable, 1 misleading) while the Gemini-corrected text had 4 errors of which 3 were "misleading" (a total elision of "totius" and a new error "eam" where the raw model was right); the authors advise against using the corrected version without the raw one. Prompt: told the model the input is the output of a handwriting network on medieval English legal Latin, "keep a similar word count", "preserve the line breaks at all costs", "keep in mind that this is medieval, not classical, Latin", no explanations. — [arXiv 2605.00977](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00977)
- Latin court hand pipeline: Claude 3.7 Sonnet corrects a Transkribus PyLaia baseline given the image cropped in 10-line chunks with red numbered baselines overlaid and a long rule-based system prompt; final WER 2-7 % in four case studies against scholarly ground truth with human review; the author reports invented plausible text (e.g., an inserted name) when input is badly corrupted; no controlled before/after numbers. — [arXiv 2507.04132](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.04132)
- Degradation cases: ByT5-based post-OCR correction and retrieval-augmented prompting lowered aggregate translation quality on medieval Latin lines because of error propagation and prompt saturation; commercial models refused 1.3-24 % of requests on garbled input. For 18th-c. Russian print, 12 multimodal LLMs showed "over-historicization" and post-OCR correction "degrades rather than improves performance". — [arXiv 2607.03836](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.03836), [arXiv 2510.06743](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.06743)
- Text-only LLM correction of 20th-c. German print (periodicals): German Mistral-7B lowered average WER while average CER "decreases or remains unchanged" for all models tested (instruction-tuned Llama-2-13B and Mistral-7B variants). — [ACL Anthology 2025.resourceful-1.26](https://aclanthology.org/2025.resourceful-1.26/)
- VLM error structure: fluent, language-prior-driven substitutions; post-OCR language-model correction improves several systems, but only by repairing the text after generation (abstract-level claim). — [arXiv 2605.27750](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.27750)
- Informal replication (non-peer-reviewed): correcting an LLM-generated draft of Bentham pages cut strict CER 2.33 % to 1.54 % (Claude Opus 5.5) and 1.66 % (Gemini 3.1 Pro); Greif's German-directory result replicated (Transkribus draft 3.67 %; Gemini 3.1 Pro 0.79 % reading the image alone and 0.75 % given image plus draft; Claude Opus 5.5 0.71 % and 0.68 %). — [GitHub issue 6547](https://github.com/Embassy-of-the-Free-Mind/sourcelibrary-v2/issues/6547)

### Inferences
- Models that helped as correctors: Claude (3.5 Sonnet) and Gemini (1.5 Pro, 2.0 Flash, 3 Pro) in most studies; GPT-4o helped less; open models and same-model correction did not help. Prompts that helped share three features: give the image and the draft, ask for a conservative edit (keep word/line count, no explanation), and state period/language ("medieval, not classical, Latin").
- Correction gains appear largest when the draft is mostly right and errors are character-level (CER about 3-10 %); there is no evidence that a correction pass rescues a draft at 30-50 % CER, and correction on VLM-generated text is the weakest case, so a correction role fits a CTC draft much better than a VLM draft.
- Risk profile: a corrector lowers measured CER but converts visible garbage into fluent wrong words; for edition-quality work this argues for flagging changed words (diff) rather than silently accepting the corrected text.

### Gaps
- No controlled before/after CER study on 17th/18th-c. Kurrent, early-modern Latin cursive, or early-modern French handwriting that I could read in full; the one Kurrent study (DHd 2026, Goethe-era hands) reports qualitative results only in the retrievable text; the rest is English handwriting, German/Russian print, and medieval Latin.
- No comparison of open-weight correctors (Qwen3-VL, Gemma 4, Medusa) on historical German/Latin HTR output; Crosilla's open-model correction results are from 2024 models.
- ACL 2025 paper's numeric tables and the "Reading or Guessing" per-model numbers were not retrieved (abstract-level claims only).

### Inferences
- ESTIMATE for a 24 GB card (RTX 4090-class, vLLM, FP8/AWQ or bf16 3-4B models, 1,000 output tokens per Kurrent page): 7-9B VLMs about 300-1,500 pages/h and 3-4B VLMs about 600-3,000 pages/h. Derivation: olmOCR-2 on an H100 about 5,000 pages/h for short English pages (assume 2-3x lower decode bandwidth on a 24 GB card and 2x longer outputs for dense Kurrent pages) and Medusa-9B about 6 lines/s (260 lines/s divided by 45) which is about 700 pages/h at 30 lines per page on an A40; METATR's unbatched A100 figures (about 5.4 s/100 words, about 250-word page about 13.5 s, about 260 pages/h) give the floor. Memory: METATR measured 17-20 GiB for 8B models at batch 1, so a 24 GB card leaves little KV-cache room in bf16 and quantisation is advisable for batching.
- Kurrent-length pages: with about 250-400 words per page and about 1,000 output tokens, API cost is dominated by output and reasoning tokens, not by the image; capping thinking is the main lever (Gemini 3 Pro measured about $9-10 per 1,000 pages at minimal thinking vs about $116 per 1,000 for Gemini 2.5 Pro with default thinking on dense pages).
- Line-level calls are not cheaper than page-level calls: per-image overhead (260-1,150 input tokens per line crop) and reasoning tokens (about 1,500 per line on GPT-5.6) make line-by-line reading of a 30-line page cost $0.03-1.00, versus about $0.005-0.04 for one whole-page call to a non-reasoning model (ESTIMATE table above); whole-page calls trade this for layout/reading-order risk.
- Cost is not the binding constraint for a few-thousand-page corpus (a 10,000-page run is about $50-500 at list prices); accuracy and licensing are. A CTC recogniser is about two orders of magnitude cheaper per page (about 640 lines/s on an A40, about 20 pages/s at 30 lines per page, derived) than a 9B VLM line recogniser.
- Prices are volatile: several Gemini prices change on 1 Dec 2026 / 1 Jan 2027 and older models are retired, which also limits reproducibility of API-based transcriptions.

### Gaps
- No measured pages/hour for 3B/7B VLMs on an RTX 4090/3090/A5000 for Kurrent-length pages; no measured Medusa line throughput on a 24 GB card (the 45x statement in Kiessling is ambiguous about CPU vs GPU, I interpreted it as GPU).
- OpenAI image-token costs for GPT-5.x/6 and Gemini 4 Argon's actual reasoning-token behaviour on handwriting were not retrieved; estimates above assume token counts.
- The ESTIMATE table uses assumed output lengths; real Kurrent pages vary (dense pages can reach 1,500+ tokens).

## 7. Data-licensing and privacy issues of sending library images and non-commercial transcriptions to an API

### Takeaway
Under paid API terms the three major providers say they do not train on API inputs by default, so sending public-domain library scans is low privacy risk; the live issues are (1) each library's own terms for its images (not checked), (2) free/unpaid tiers (Google's free tier uses content to improve products and allows human review, although its terms apply the paid-tier data terms to users in the EEA, Switzerland and the UK, and a client distributed to EEA users must use Paid Services only), (3) CC BY-NC transcriptions (non-commercial use rules and what happens to derived/trained artefacts), and (4) "no competing models" clauses (Anthropic, Google; OpenAI's clause not verified) if API outputs are used as training labels for a model you release.

### Cited Findings
- Anthropic Commercial Terms: "Anthropic may not train models on Customer Content from Services"; the Customer "owns its Outputs" and Anthropic assigns its rights in Outputs; section D.4 prohibits using the Services "to build a competing product or service, including to train competing AI models"; retention is governed by a separate DPA (not retrieved). The vision documentation adds that image uploads are ephemeral and not used to train models. The API data-retention page (Oct 2026) states retained data is "never used for model training without your express permission" and that prompts and outputs are not retained by default, except for the "Covered Models" (Claude Fable 5/5.1 and Mythos 5/5.1) which require 30-day retention and are not ZDR-eligible; the Batch API (the 50 % discount route) keeps jobs for 29 days and is not ZDR-eligible, Files API uploads persist until deleted, and content flagged by safety systems may be kept up to 2 years even under ZDR. — [Commercial Terms](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms), [vision docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/vision), [API and data retention](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/api-and-data-retention)
- Google Gemini API terms: Unpaid Services - Google uses submitted content and responses "to provide, improve, and develop" its products, human reviewers "may read, annotate, and process" API input and output, and users should not submit sensitive, confidential or personal information; Paid Services - Google states it does not use prompts or responses to improve its products and logs them for a limited period only to detect and prevent violations; "You may not use the Services to develop models that compete with the Services"; for users in the EEA, Switzerland and the UK the paid-services data terms apply to all services and only Paid Services may be used when making API clients available to EEA users. The pricing page confirms free tier "Content used to improve our products" vs paid "not used". The logs-policy page adds that project logs on billing-enabled projects are kept for a default maximum of 55 days (shortenable to 7, 14 or 28 days in AI Studio), are not used for product improvement unless a dataset is deliberately shared, and are separate from abuse-monitoring logs whose retention period that page does not state. — [Gemini API terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms), [pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing), [logs policy](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/logs-policy)
- OpenAI API data controls: data sent to the API "is not used to train or improve OpenAI models" by default; abuse-monitoring logs are kept up to 30 days; Zero Data Retention is subject to prior approval; EU data residency (eu.api.openai.com, EEA and Switzerland) supports regional storage and processing but requires ZDR, Modified Abuse Monitoring, or similar controls. The business Services Agreement was not retrievable (HTTP 403); search results indicate that OpenAI's consumer/rest-of-world and EU Terms of Use list using Output to develop competing models among prohibited conduct (wording not verified by me). — [OpenAI data controls](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/your-data), [OpenAI RoW terms (per search result)](https://openai.com/en-GB/policies/row-terms-of-use/)
- Anthropic first-party "US-only inference" costs a 1.1x multiplier; regional/multi-region endpoints on Bedrock and Google Cloud carry a 10 % premium. Mistral OCR 3 offers self-hosting for organisations with strict privacy requirements (contact Mistral); no EU-hosting statement was found on the pages retrieved. — [Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing), [Mistral OCR 3](https://mistral.ai/news/mistral-ocr-3)
- Precedent practice: the Humphries team used only out-of-copyright public-domain documents and relied on API terms that, in their words, precluded "OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google from retaining or using this data to train their models in future" (2024 statement); they kept the test set private partly to avoid contamination of future models. — [arXiv 2411.03340](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.03340)
- Project posture (Leibniz Legible README and SPECS section 7): code Apache-2.0; GWLB scans carry Public Domain Mark 1.0; transcriptions are CC BY 4.0; ground truth is deposited in two channels, open (CC BY 4.0) and NC-derived (CC BY-NC 4.0: Potsdam Reihe IV PDFs, GWLB repository PDFs), with NC-licensed sources kept in a quarantined bucket excluded from CC BY releases and the UI; a model fine-tuned from the PHILIUMM checkpoint (published CC BY-NC 4.0 since 15 Oct 2026) is CC BY-NC 4.0, one with no NC input CC BY 4.0; the SPECS plan bounded LLM passes through the Anthropic Batch API with LLM output stored only as `status='corrected'` with model version. — [project README](https://github.com/marchofhares/leibnizlegible/blob/main/README.md), [project SPECS.md](https://github.com/marchofhares/leibnizlegible/blob/main/SPECS.md)
- CC BY-NC 4.0 (read): "NonCommercial means not primarily intended for or directed towards commercial advantage or monetary compensation"; the grant is to "reproduce and Share the Licensed Material ... for NonCommercial purposes only"; "Adapted Material" is material derived from the licensed material by translation, alteration or transformation. — [CC BY-NC 4.0 legal code](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/legalcode.en)
- NC and SA terms already appear in HTR training data: CHURRO-DS combines CC BY-NC-SA, CC BY-NC-ND and research-only corpora but is re-released as CC BY-SA 4.0 "to comply with these licenses"; the LightOnOCR-3 German fine-tune is released CC BY-SA 4.0 because one training source is CC BY-SA 4.0. — [arXiv 2509.19768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19768), [HF card](https://huggingface.co/b-straub/LightOnOCR-3-1B-Historic-German-Handwriting)
- Open-weight terms can also reach outputs: under the Gemma Terms (Gemma 3) a model trained on Gemma-generated synthetic data is a "Model Derivative" bound by the same restrictions; the Qwen Research License permits derivatives only for non-commercial purposes. — [Gemma terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/terms), [Qwen Research License](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct/blob/main/LICENSE)
- EU DSM Directive Art. 14 (as summarised by commentators): once protection of a work of visual art has expired, reproductions of it are not subject to copyright or related rights unless the reproduction is original; commentators disagree whether the provision covers all manuscripts and old documents held by heritage institutions, and the German implementation was not retrieved. — [COMMUNIA report](https://communia-association.org/2024/02/09/the-post-dsm-copyright-report-public-domain-and-cultural-heritage-rights/), [European Copyright Society comment](https://international.vlex.com/vid/comment-of-the-european-847722052)
- Reproducibility angle: provider tables list retired models (Claude Opus 4.1, Sonnet 4, Haiku 3.5 retired except on cloud platforms; Gemini 3 Flash Preview marked legacy), so API transcriptions cannot be regenerated with the same model later. — [Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing), [Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing)

### Inferences
- For public-domain scans, a paid tier with default no-training terms (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google paid) is the baseline; free/unpaid tiers (and Google's invite-only Contributor Tier) should be excluded because content may be used for product improvement and human review (Google's terms say the paid-tier data terms apply to users in the EEA, Switzerland and the UK even on unpaid quota, but verify the current text before relying on that). Logs are still kept (OpenAI abuse monitoring up to 30 days; Google project logs up to 55 days by default; Anthropic keeps Batch jobs for 29 days and requires 30-day retention for its top Fable/Mythos models), so "no training" is not "no retention"; ZDR (where eligible; not for Anthropic Batch or Fable/Mythos) or self-hosting (open VLMs, Mistral self-host) is the stricter option, and the 50 % batch discounts partly trade against retention.
- Library images: terms of use are a separate contractual layer from copyright; Art. 14 DSM helps for faithful reproductions of public-domain works in the EU but its reach over manuscripts is contested; each library's terms (and any "no AI/third-party processing" clause) must be checked per collection (I did not check any).
- Non-commercial transcriptions: the CC BY-NC restriction attaches to the licensee's use; putting NC transcriptions into prompts or few-shot examples of a commercial API is a licence-scope question that depends on whether the overall use stays "not primarily intended for commercial advantage", whereas training and releasing a model or dataset derived from NC transcriptions under CC BY/Apache-2.0 is the higher-risk step because NC (and any SA) terms may follow the derivative; either way I would treat NC-licensed transcriptions as unsuitable seed data for an openly licensed release unless the rights holders agree (not legal advice).
- For this project specifically: the images (PDM 1.0) and the open-channel ground truth (CC BY 4.0) raise no licence obstacle to API use; the NC bucket is the open question, because the quarantine rule is written against releases and the UI, not against sending NC text or image-text pairs to a third-party processor, and Anthropic's Batch API (the planned route and the 50 % discount) keeps jobs for 29 days and is not ZDR-eligible; recording an explicit rule for NC-bucket egress (allow for private evaluation prompts, or forbid) is a project decision I cannot resolve from the sources (not legal advice).
- Using API-generated transcriptions as pseudo-labels to train a released model collides with the "no competing models" clauses of Anthropic and Google (OpenAI's consumer/RoW terms are reported to contain an equivalent clause, not verified against the business agreement); whether a historical-HTR model "competes" is untested, so the conservative route is to use API outputs only for flagging/review and to train released models on human-verified text.

### Gaps
- No library terms of use were retrieved (Staatsbibliothek zu Berlin, BSB, ÖNB, e-rara/e-manuscripta, ZB Zürich, Staatsarchiv Zürich etc.); the search found only generic library guidance on licensed-content AI uploads.
- OpenAI's business Services Agreement (competing-models clause, ownership of Output) and Google's abuse-monitoring retention period were not retrieved; Vertex AI, Azure OpenAI and Bedrock EU-region data terms were not researched.
- Legal propagation of NC/SA/CC BY terms to model weights, EU AI Act obligations for GPAI/downstream users, and the German implementation of DSM Art. 14 were not researched.
