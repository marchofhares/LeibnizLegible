# Adapt, decode, rebuild: cutting Leibniz's HTR errors

No open dataset contains German in Leibniz's hand or in his secretaries' hands, and verified open Kurrent ground truth for 1600–1750 totals about 1,600 pages, most of it under contested licences, so the Kurrent track's realistic gains come from levers the project controls: measuring how much of the 37 % and 49 % CER is segmentation, adapting the best reader to a few hundred verified own-hand lines (published reductions of 25 % with 16 lines and 50 % with 256 lines for new writers, up to 82 % for Kurrent-like scripts), and piloting two new Apache-2.0 bases, kraken's page-aware "party" model and PP-OCRv6, the latter only after upgrading from kraken 7.0.3 to 7.1. For the Latin/French model, the most reliable add-on is beam-search decoding with a character 6-gram language model, which kraken 7.0.3 no longer ships and which gained 0.5–1.0 CER points on comparable datasets at 8–10 % CER; a PP-OCRv6 base (24–33 % relative gains in matched fine-tunes on new manuscripts, less expected at the project's data scale) and an in-domain ByT5 corrector (10–40 % relative, an estimate) come next. PHILIUMM's relicensing of its recognition and segmentation models to CC BY-NC 4.0 on 15 October 2026 (its layout model carries the same licence), while its dataset stays CC BY 4.0, makes a rebuild from FoNDUE-GD or PP-OCRv6 on that dataset the one step that restores a clean licence, tests a stronger base and creates the document-disjoint test set the 7.95 % figure never had. Vision-language models still read Leibniz's Latin/French four to ten times worse than the fine-tuned kraken model, and early-modern German pages at roughly 35–60 % CER, so their defensible role is an image-grounded second reader whose disagreements route human review, never a label source for released models, since Anthropic's and Google's terms forbid using their services to train competing models. Several tempting resources, including the Bullinger set the project already uses, the Leopold I letters, the Dresden diaries and Hugging Face sets tagged "MIT" with no stated basis, need a licence decision before they enter a CC BY training mix.

## Open Kurrent ground truth is thin, older than Leibniz's letters and never in his hand

The project's 37 % and 49 % are domain-gap numbers, not signs of a weak reader. The Bern TrOCR card reports **5.42 % test CER**, but on an automatic split "evaluated against the same hands" of about 1.58 million words weighted toward Swiss archives ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/dh-unibe/trocr-kurrent-XVI-XVII)); the same model scored **43.5 % on 164 lines of a 1505 Lübeck Hanserezess** ([jwidmer evaluation](https://huggingface.co/datasets/jwidmer/kurrent-hanse-xvi-test-lines-with-evaluation)) and 7.4 % on the regular Saxon chancery hand of Dresden diary K.117 in the project's own smoke test ([project report](https://github.com/marchofhares/LeibnizLegible/blob/d3391c2112bc099571aa11492d6d52d666fa338c/reports/kurrent/bootstrap-candidates.md)). In this regime in-domain volume dominates: TrOCR fine-tuned on 17th-century German reached **53 % CER with 30 annotated pages and 9.2 % with 500** ([arXiv 2410.24034](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.24034)). At the project's average of about 68 lines per German page ([STATUS](https://github.com/marchofhares/leibnizlegible/blob/main/STATUS.md)), the 55,000 aligned lines now training amount to roughly 800 pages (my arithmetic), so the fine-tune sits where label quality matters more than the choice of base, and its score on aligner-placed validation lines says nothing yet about the honest test. The census splits the problem in two: of 2,173 German edition pieces, **1,430 are classed as other people's hands and only 82 as Leibniz's own**, with 18,056 recognised lines ([census](https://github.com/marchofhares/LeibnizLegible/blob/d3391c2112bc099571aa11492d6d52d666fa338c/reports/kurrent/census.md)). The 37 % is mainly a problem of correspondents' diversity; the 49 % is a single-writer problem, and they call for different levers.

Verified open page-level ground truth inside 1600–1750 adds up to about **1,611 pages**: 42 pages of Dresden court diaries (1653–1673), **271 pages of Emperor Leopold I's autograph letters** (1655–1667, published December 2025) and **1,298 page rows of 17th-century Hanseatic recesses** (July 2026) ([Dresden K.113](https://zenodo.org/records/15303398); [Leopold I](https://zenodo.org/records/17911243); [Hanse XVII](https://huggingface.co/datasets/fgho/hanse-kurrent-xvii-rawxml)). The HTR-United catalogue lists no German manuscript set later than 1673 ([htr-united.yml](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/HTR-United/htr-united/master/htr-united.yml)). No open dataset holds German in Leibniz's hand, none labels lines by scribe, so nothing identifies the hands of his secretaries Hodann and Eckhart, and the only open Leibniz-hand ground truth is PHILIUMM's Latin and French set ([PHILIUMM dataset](https://zenodo.org/records/21622297)). The closed world is far larger: Greifswald's Transkribus model "German_Kurrent_17th-18th" was trained on 8,915 pages from about 1,500 writers and "The German Giant I" on 86,345 pages, but neither page states a licence or says the data is public, so they serve only as comparison baselines ([Greifswald model](https://www.transkribus.org/models/german-kurrent-17th-18th-century); [German Giant I](https://www.transkribus.org/models/the-german-giant-i)). Much of the clean pool is already inside the bases the project would use. The Bern reader's 1.58 million words came from Zürich's Stillstandsprotokolle and Ratsmanuale, Königsfelden, Einsiedeln and Greifswald's university archives ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/dh-unibe/trocr-kurrent-XVI-XVII)); party's German training lists FONDUE-DE-MSS-16/18/19/20, Dresden diary K.113, the Leopold I set, mkn-kurrent-gt and Gantner's 19th-century lines ([Zenodo 20642057](https://zenodo.org/records/20642057)); and kraken describes its PP-OCRv6 bases as trained on "essentially the entirety of openly accessible historical ATR datasets" ([kraken changelog](https://kraken.re/7.0/changelog.html)). Feeding these sets back mostly guards against forgetting; genuinely new signal has to come from the project's own aligned and verified lines, from editions nobody has aligned yet, and from the licence-unclear sets once their status is settled. On relevance, the notes judge (without measuring hand similarity) that individual mixed-script hands such as Leopold I, the Tübingen consilia and the Zürich pastors come closest to Leibniz, that chancery sets such as Dresden, Hanse and Bozen teach generic letterforms and Early Modern German spelling, and that 19th-century pools such as dh-unibe's 622,298-line Kurrent-XIX set teach the wrong letterforms under a bare "MIT" tag ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/dh-unibe/image-text_kurrent-xix-lines)).

The most valuable additions may therefore be editions rather than datasets, since the project already mints lines by aligning edition text to images. The Tübingen consilia of 1659–1665 pair a public-domain IIIF manifest of 553 canvases with a full Transkribus transcription in German laced with Latin legal phrases; the object page does not say whether the text was hand-corrected, though UB Mannheim used the volume as ground truth for its German model ([manifest](https://opendigi.ub.uni-tuebingen.de/opendigi/UAT_084_13/manifest); [text view](https://opendigi.ub.uni-tuebingen.de/opendigi/UAT_084_13/ocr); [Mannheim wiki](https://github.com/UB-Mannheim/kraken/wiki/Training-German-Handwriting)). The 1665 Dresden diary exists as a complete manual TEI transcription under CC BY 4.0, and SLUB's scans carry the Public Domain Mark ([Zenodo 14932508](https://zenodo.org/records/14932508); [Zenodo 15303398](https://zenodo.org/records/15303398)); aligning that text oneself sidesteps the NC-SA ambiguity of the packaged Dresden ground truth (my inference). The 1653–56 and 1673 diary texts are a trap: they are HTR output corrected by Claude 3.7 Sonnet and checked by hand, which their authors call "nicht immer zeichengenau, sondern sinnerhaltend" ([Zenodo 15228309](https://zenodo.org/records/15228309)). The closest analogue in kind, the diaries of Prince Christian II of Anhalt-Bernburg (about 17,400 pages "mostly in the prince's own hand", switching between German and "above all French, Italian and Latin"), remains a lead, because its edition, portal and PURL returned HTTP 503 and no licence was found ([HAB project page](https://www.hab.de/en/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/17000); [idw release](https://idw-online.de/en/news628178)). Pietist and scholarly letter collections are closed: the Francke portal allows only non-commercial use, and the Spener and Gottsched correspondences are print editions ([Francke terms](https://digital.francke-halle.de/download/pdf/252404.pdf); [SAW Spener](https://www.saw-leipzig.de/en/research/projects/edition-der-briefe-philipp-jakob-speners-1635-1705-vor-allem-aus-der-berliner-zeit-1691-1705)).

The three in-period sets with real volume each need a licence conversation. The Leopold I deposit, private letters in "German kurrent and Latin cursive" by a hand its makers call "difficult to read", states CC BY 4.0 in Zenodo's metadata, ships a LICENSE.MD containing the CC BY-SA 4.0 text, and leaves image rights with the Haus-, Hof- und Staatsarchiv and the Oberösterreichisches Landesarchiv; its abbreviations are "quietly resolved" and punctuation modernised, so its lines behave like edition text ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/HTR-School-Vienna/2025--early-modern-german/main/README.md); [LICENSE.MD](https://zenodo.org/records/17911243/files/LICENSE.MD)). The Hanse sets carry a bare `license: mit` with no rights statement for images drawn from several archives, although the matching Transkribus model shows what such data supports: 947 pages and 5.08 % validation CER ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/fgho/hanse-kurrent-xvii-rawxml); [Transkribus Hanserezesse v.5](https://www.transkribus.org/models/hanserezesse-17th-century-v5)). The Dresden records say CC BY 4.0 in their metadata and CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 in their descriptions ([Zenodo 15303243](https://zenodo.org/records/15303243)). Emails to the HTR School Vienna, FGHO and Stefan Beckert cost nothing and could turn about 1,600 pages into usable data; the same route is the only one to the Greifswald and Bern pools behind the closed models, since the notes found no other release channel. Two of these sets already reach the project indirectly through party, which also disqualifies them as fair tests of that model.

## Writer adaptation and two Apache-2.0 bases are the Kurrent levers with evidence

Recent benchmarks argue against chasing architectures. On BullingerDB's 499,222 lines of 16th-century Latin and Early New High German, PyLaia with 4.8 M parameters reached **9.2 % CER against 9.1 % for a 334 M-parameter TrOCR** ([arXiv 2605.30235](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30235)); on READ 2016's Early Modern German lines, HTR-VT (3.9 %), DAN and VAN (both 4.1 %) sit within half a point of each other ([arXiv 2409.08573](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.08573)); and a fine-tuned 15.9 M-parameter PP-OCRv6 beat a Qwen3.5-9B line recogniser on Latin, 8.70 % to 13.07 %, while running about 45 times faster ([arXiv 2609.20064](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20064)). CER moves with broad pretraining, adaptation to the target hand and clean inputs, and the candidates below are judged on those terms:

**Party** is the most promising new base to run inside kraken. A Swin encoder feeds a ~40 M-parameter byte-level Llama decoder prompted with each line's box or baseline, so it consumes eScriptorium-style segmentation unchanged, and per-line language tokens steer output on pages where German, Latin and French alternate. Fine-tuning runs through the separate `party` package (`party compile`, whose datasets are not kraken's, then `party train --load-from-repo … --prompt-mode curves|boxes`, with `--freeze-encoder` to "substantially" cut memory), and inference runs through `kraken … ocr`; the model is 518 MB and cannot be quantised ([party README](https://github.com/mittagessen/party)). The notes estimate anywhere from parity to a 10–30 % relative improvement after fine-tuning, with high uncertainty, on the rented 24 GB card; its memory use at given batch sizes is undocumented, and Turing-generation cards such as the 1660 Ti lack native bf16, which party recommends (background knowledge, not verified in the notes). Its 9.10 % German figure mixes manuscripts and print, so it predicts nothing about Leibniz.

The PP-OCRv6 family the project already lists needs **kraken ≥ 7.1.0** (released 5 August 2026) and cannot run under 7.0.3; eScriptorium's development branch already pins `kraken~=7.1.0` with the `dfine-kraken` layout plug-in ([kraken changelog](https://kraken.re/main/changelog.html); [eScriptorium requirements](https://gitlab.com/scripta/escriptorium/-/raw/develop/app/requirements.txt)). The upgrade unlocks `ketos train --arch ppocrv6` with `--load`, `--resize union` and `--freeze-backbone N` against forgetting ([kraken 7.1 training docs](https://kraken.re/7.1/user_guide/training_recognition.html)), and moves the German track onto a CTC model, where language-model decoding and calibrated confidences become available. In few-shot tests PP-OCRv6 improved only after 40–80 target lines (on one manuscript not within 80) where kraken's CRNN improved with 20, yet it had the lower absolute CER from 40 lines on all four manuscripts ([arXiv 2609.20064](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20064)). The Nordic bases are permissive but linguistically distant, and the notes put their gain over continuing from the already-adapted Bern model at 0–10 % at best.

Two further Kurrent bases carry ShareAlike. UB Mannheim's kraken `german_handwriting` model was trained on the Tübingen consilia, Greifswald minutes, READ Bozen and the Basel Urfehdenbuch, publishes no CER and fine-tunes easily on 6 GB; it is an ensemble member, not a likely winner against a 55,000-line fine-tune ([Zenodo 7933463](https://zenodo.org/records/7933463)). **LightOnOCR-3-1B-Historic-German-Handwriting** is the only open model that reads whole pages without segmentation and marks deletions ⟦…⟧, insertions ⟨…⟩ and illegible spots ▨, which is what Leibniz's drafts need ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/b-straub/LightOnOCR-3-1B-Historic-German-Handwriting)). A few-shot run with 28 pages and 60 steps (about an hour) halved its CER on an unseen hard corpus from 44 % to 25 %. Training peaked at 88.6 GB, so a 24 GB fine-tune needs batch 1, gradient checkpointing and probably LoRA, and the vision encoder must run in bf16 because f16 overflows into strings of "!!!!". It pays off only if the project produces page-level diplomatic ground truth with revision marks, and every derivative stays CC BY-SA.

For Leibniz's own hand, the best-supported lever is plain fine-tuning of the strongest general model on a few hundred verified lines with augmentation. On a large real-world benchmark this cut CER by **about 25 % with 16 lines and 50 % with 256 lines from a new writer**, with the largest reductions, **up to 82 %**, for "distinct yet to some extent source-like" scripts "such as Kurrent"; easy, source-like hands gained at most 10 %, and few-line runs could overfit by up to 15 % ([Kohút & Hradiš](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06308)). Writers already present in training gain much less: 6 % without and 15 % with augmentation in the same study, and 3.7–9.8 % relative for Bullinger's frequent writers ([Scius-Bertrand et al.](https://arodes.hes-so.ch/record/13180/files/Scius-Bertrand_2023_Bullinger_dataset.pdf)). The distinction matters, because if the 55,000 aligned lines already include Leibniz's own-hand pieces, verification mainly removes alignment noise and the smaller figures apply. The notes extrapolate **a further 20–40 % relative reduction on the own-hand test from 100–300 verified lines**, an estimate whose optimistic anchor is Kurrent's large published gains. Two conditions keep that number honest: a document-disjoint test, since Kiessling's few-shot sets are same-page and inflate gains ([arXiv 2609.20064](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20064)), and training lines seeded from copyright-expired edition text or fresh transcription rather than from the GWLB's CC BY-NC transcriptions, which the project reserves for evaluation (my inference from its quarantine rule, [README](https://github.com/marchofhares/leibnizlegible)). Own-hand pieces covered by the edition text the project already aligns can supply training lines while the GWLB letters of 1708–1716 stay the test. Writer-style embeddings are no shortcut: the Writer Style Block's best writer-dependent gain was 9.22 %, and it did worse than baseline on new writers ([arXiv 2302.06318](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06318)).

Revised drafts raise a prior question: how much of the 49 % is segmentation? On medieval charters, line-level CER rose from **12.0 % on manual lines to 23.3–80.1 % on lines from three automatic detectors**, mostly through merged lines, and unifying polygon labels cut Bozen CER from 27.3 % to 8.8 % ([Boillet et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.12346)); on a Medieval Greek scholar's hand, automatic polygons more than tripled CER, from 2.69 % to 8.90 % ([KONVENS 2026](https://www.korpuslab.uni-hamburg.de/en/konvens2026/121.pdf)). Recognising a few dozen honest-test pages on hand-corrected and on automatic lines measures this directly. If the gap is large, kraken 7's layout plug-in `dfine_kraken` (Apache-2.0; [GitHub](https://github.com/mittagessen/dfine_kraken)) or a YOLO region model of the YALTAi kind, which beat kraken's segmenter on small training sets ([JDMDH](https://jdmdh.episciences.org/9806)), can be trained on corrected draft pages. Abbreviations are the other structural cost: on a heavily abbreviated Latin manuscript, a character model scored 4.55–4.89 % CER on graphemic transcriptions but 9.69–10.59 % when trained and scored on expansions, against different reference strings ([Camps et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03450)). Edition-aligned lines teach the model to expand abbreviations silently (assuming the Academy text expands them, which the notes did not verify), so the policy for own-hand ground truth, graphemic with a later expansion step or expanded, should be fixed before verification starts, together with a rule for strike-throughs and insertions.

The project's millions of unlabelled line crops are a weaker lever than they look. Self-supervised pretraining beat training from scratch at 100 and 1,000 labelled lines but not at 10,000 (2.75 % from scratch against 2.91 % and 3.60 % for two SSL methods), and transfer from a related labelled model won throughout with 2.27 % ([Kišš & Hradiš](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00420)); the notes estimate under 5 % relative for days of 24 GB compute. Synthetic lines from a handwriting generator were on average worse than real-data pretraining ([Pippi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.02593)), so the 160,000 CC BY synthetic 18th-century Kurrent and copperplate lines on Zenodo ([Zenodo 22093255](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22093255)) or pangoline renderings (Apache-2.0; [GitHub](https://github.com/mittagessen/pangoline)) are at most a patch for rare signs. Confidence-filtered self-training is the better use of unlabelled pages: thresholded pseudo-labels took a word recogniser from 25.41 % to 12.37 % CER, against 16.65 % with all pseudo-labels, figures the notes flag for checking against the PDF ([Wolf & Fink](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.03149)); kraken already divides logits by a temperature, and PyLaia's authors found T = 2 a robust setting for calibrating line confidence ([kraken models.py](https://github.com/mittagessen/kraken/blob/7.0.3/kraken/lib/models.py); [arXiv 2404.18722](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18722)). Language-model decoding helps little at this error level (on POPP, at 16.5 % CER, a character LM bought only 0.40 points, [arXiv 2404.18722](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18722)), and the TrOCR reader has no CTC output to rescore; no LM-fusion result for TrOCR on historical handwriting was found. Its analogue is n-best voting, which cut a single-writer Latin TrOCR's CER from 1.86 % to 1.60 % with five augmented models ([arXiv 2508.11499](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11499)).

## The Latin/French 7.95 % is optimistic, and a clean rebuild fixes licence and evaluation together

The 7.95 % (95 % CI 7.49–8.46) reproduces PHILIUMM's claimed 8.33 % on the model's 1,878-line validation split, which is Latin and French by construction, drawn from manually corrected lines, pre-segmented and dewarped ([B1 report](https://zenodo.org/records/22782817)). Three features make it a ceiling rather than a forecast. No test set was ever created ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/DenisaBumba/htr_leibniz_dataset_v1)). The split is random rather than document-disjoint, and random splits flatter models: kraken scored 4.7 % on CATMuS Medieval's random split and 13.1 % on its out-of-domain split ([CATMuS paper](https://inria.hal.science/hal-04453952)). And the dataset card says part of the clean data "was later used to train FoNDUE-GD-v2", whose version 2.1 lists 13,049 PHILIUMM lines without saying whether the 27 validation files were excluded ([FoNDUE-GD 2.1](https://zenodo.org/records/21536798)). Production pages add segmentation error on top. Every lever below should therefore be scored on a document-disjoint test set with a single scorer: kraken 7.0.3 changed `ketos test` to normalise predictions as well as ground truth, so CERs from different versions are not comparable ([kraken releases](https://github.com/mittagessen/kraken/releases)).

The licence ground shifted on **15 October 2026**, when the records of PHILIUMM's recognition model and kraken segmentation model changed to **CC BY-NC 4.0**; its RF-DETR layout model carries the same licence, while the dataset remains CC BY 4.0 ([HTR model](https://zenodo.org/records/21457538); [segmentation](https://zenodo.org/records/21537859); [layout](https://zenodo.org/records/21514239); [dataset](https://zenodo.org/records/21622297)). The project's README already treats any model fine-tuned from the PHILIUMM checkpoint as CC BY-NC and keeps the transcriptions CC BY by agreement, but the B1 report of 16 September still calls the model CC BY 4.0 and needs a correction ([README](https://github.com/marchofhares/leibnizlegible); [B1 report](https://zenodo.org/records/22782817)); whether copies obtained under the earlier grant remain usable under it is a legal question the notes did not settle. The practical answer is to stop inheriting and rebuild. PHILIUMM published its recipe, 43,372 noisy aligned lines at learning rate 2e-4 and then 20,139 clean lines at 5e-5, which took FoNDUE-GD_v2 from **15.04 % to 10.86 % to 8.33 % CER** ([Zenodo 21457538](https://zenodo.org/records/21457538)); the same run from **FoNDUE-GD (CC BY 4.0)** on the CC BY dataset plus the project's aligned lines should land near today's model without the NC grant. FoNDUE-GD's Latin and French variants report own-validation CER of 8.58 % and 7.64 % against 8.90 % for the generic model, values the notes extracted from the model files ([Zenodo 14399779](https://zenodo.org/records/14399779)). The lineage is not pristine, since FoNDUE-GD lists MEGV-FR-MSS-18, whose transcriptions are CC BY-NC, among its training sets ([FoNDUE-GD 2.1](https://zenodo.org/records/21536798)); the rebuild relies on an upstream label just as party and PP-OCRv6 do, and what it removes is the explicit NC licence on the checkpoint itself. The dataset's licence should be re-checked and its Zenodo version pinned before each release.

The rebuild is also the cheapest moment to trial a stronger base. After matched fine-tuning on CATMuS, **PP-OCRv6 medium** (Apache-2.0, 15.86 M parameters) beat kraken's CRNN on held-out competition manuscripts, French 5.01 → 3.36 %, Latin 12.61 → 8.70 % and Spanish 10.47 → 7.99 %, a **24–33 % relative reduction**, whereas from scratch on the full CATMuS corpus the CRNN matched or beat it (French 4.32 against 4.94 %, Latin 11.00 against 12.17 %) ([arXiv 2609.20064](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20064)). Pretraining carries the gain, and its edge shrinks as target data grows, in one German medieval study from 85 % at 2 pages to 39 % at 32 pages ([Reul et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.07661)). With 60,000-plus lines of one writer the notes therefore expect less than 24–33 %; I would budget 5–15 % relative and treat more as upside, an estimate. Party (Latin 7.47 %, French 7.49 % on its held-out splits) adds page context and language tokens for Leibniz's language switching but needs the 24 GB card ([Zenodo 20642057](https://zenodo.org/records/20642057)). TRIDIS v2's MIT kraken model is semi-diplomatic medieval material trained without Unicode normalisation, a codec mismatch with the NFD-trained FoNDUE family ([Zenodo 13862096](https://zenodo.org/records/13862096)).

More open Latin/French ground truth will not move the needle much, because nearly all of it is already inside FoNDUE-GD. HTR-United's French handwriting sets for 1600–1800 total about 20,500 lines, about 58,000 with the Basel "Crimes et châtiments" sets, and no Latin handwriting set falls in 1600–1800 except the Leibniz material itself ([catalog.json](https://htr-united.github.io/htr-united/catalog.json); [FoNDUE-GD 2.1](https://zenodo.org/records/21536798)); the project's 63,000 PHILIUMM lines and roughly 297,000 aligned lines exceed that entire pool ([B2–C2 report](https://zenodo.org/records/22782819)). Generalisation research points the same way: across 336 out-of-distribution cases, textual divergence mattered more than visual divergence ([Garrido-Muñoz & Calvo-Zaragoza](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025/papers/Garrido-Munoz_On_the_Generalization_of_Handwritten_Text_Recognition_Models_CVPR_2025_paper.pdf)), and in a 17th-century French project a smaller, lexically closer base beat a larger one, 5.88 % against 7.87 % ([Couture et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.11146)). Leibniz-era scholarly correspondence is locked: BEOL's Bernoulli data defaults to CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, Newton Project transcriptions are CC BY-NC-ND 3.0, Huygens' manuscripts sit behind Brill's paywall, and no licence was found for Kircher's letters ([DaSCH API](https://api.dasch.swiss/admin/projects/shortcode/0801); [Newton Project](https://newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/xml/NATP00242); [Brill](https://primarysources.brillonline.com/browse/codices-hugeniani); [GATE](https://gate.unigre.it/mediawiki/index.php/Athanasius_Kircher_Correspondence_%28AKC%29)). The aligned-text fine-tune the project already plans is itself the biggest lever on paper, which sets the baseline for everything else. Applying data-scaling curves of 10–16 % CER reduction per doubling, the notes estimate that adding 297,000 aligned lines to 63,000 (about 2.5 doublings) could take 7.95 % to **5.1–6.1 %** if aligned lines were worth as much as clean ones, or 6.4–7.0 % if each were worth half, an estimate with weak support drawn from few-page German medieval regimes ([Reul et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.07661)). Alignment quality is a lever in its own right: CTC-based re-alignment with confidence filtering lowered PyLaia's CER by 1.1 and 0.7 points on Bullinger's corrected pages, and "weaker models yield more accurate alignments" (88.5 against 86.1 % and 89.1 against 77.3 % line accuracy) ([Peer et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07904)), which argues against aligning with the same strong model that then trains on the output. Simply dropping flagged lines did not help (9.41–9.71 % against 9.13 %), and noisy labels inflated measured error on affected lines from 12.89 % to 19.47 % ([Jungo et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.09037)), one more reason to score only on verified lines.

Because the 7.95 % is measured on hand-corrected lines, segmentation is the unmeasured share of production error; published gaps between correct and automatic lines run from about 1.1× to 4.2× ([Boillet et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2203.12346); [KONVENS 2026](https://www.korpuslab.uni-hamburg.de/en/konvens2026/121.pdf)). The NC relicensing reaches this stage too, since PHILIUMM's segmentation and layout models are now CC BY-NC; retraining kraken's segmenter on the CC BY PHILIUMM PAGE data, or training `dfine_kraken` (Apache-2.0) on it, keeps the layout stage clean (my inference). Baselines and polygons perform comparably as line representations ([READ D6.11](https://readcoop.eu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/READ_D6.11_Line_and_Word_Segmentation_Tools_P2.pdf)), and party accepts either.

## A character language model is the surest add-on, worth half a point to a point

kraken 7.0.3 decodes greedily: its `ctc_decoder.py` exports only `greedy_decoder`, whereas the 6.0.3 tag still defined a beam decoder, and the main branch contains no KenLM, pyctcdecode or lexicon code ([7.0.3 source](https://github.com/mittagessen/kraken/blob/7.0.3/kraken/lib/ctc_decoder.py); [6.0.3 source](https://github.com/mittagessen/kraken/blob/6.0.3/kraken/lib/ctc_decoder.py)). Restoring beam search with a character n-gram LM is the best-documented gain available. Teklia's PyLaia study built a **6-gram character LM from each dataset's training transcriptions alone** and decoded with a fixed weight of 1.5: Himanis (medieval Latin and French) fell from **9.87 % to 8.87 %**, HOME-Alcar (Latin cartularies) from 8.35 % to 7.85 % and Belfort (19th-century French) from 10.54 % to 9.52 %, with WER falling 3–5 points; across 12 datasets the average was **−12 % relative CER**, and the LM hurt only where CER was already below 2 % (Esposalles 0.76 → 1.04 %) ([Tarride et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18722)). Those LMs saw 18,000–60,000 lines; one built on the project's aligned lines and the edition text is far larger and in-domain, so the notes estimate **−0.5 to −1.0 points, perhaps −1.5**. These are estimates, and the absolute gain shrinks once the aligned fine-tune has lowered CER.

Integration takes days, not a research programme. `TorchSeqRecognizer.forward()` returns per-line softmax matrices of shape (N, W, C) after dividing logits by a temperature, label 0 of kraken's codec is the CTC blank, and the constructor accepts a custom `decoder` function ([models.py](https://github.com/mittagessen/kraken/blob/7.0.3/kraken/lib/models.py); [codec.py](https://github.com/mittagessen/kraken/blob/main/kraken/lib/codec.py)). A beam decoder written as that callable, returning label, start, end and confidence like `greedy_decoder`, keeps the character positions and confidences eScriptorium uses. The engine can be flashlight-text (GitHub LICENSE MIT, PyPI metadata BSD) or torchaudio's `ctc_decoder` with `lexicon=None`, the route PyLaia takes, although whether current torchaudio releases still ship it is unverified; pyctcdecode (Apache-2.0, last release January 2023) suits word-level LMs, and Word Beam Search (MIT) constrains output to a lexicon with a free mode for numbers and punctuation ([flashlight/text](https://github.com/flashlight/text); [torchaudio decoder](https://pytorch.org/audio/0.12.0/models.decoder.html); [pyctcdecode](https://github.com/kensho-technologies/pyctcdecode); [CTCWordBeamSearch](https://github.com/githubharald/CTCWordBeamSearch)). LMs are built with KenLM, mostly LGPL, via `lmplz --order 6`, following PyLaia's documented token and lexicon files ([KenLM](https://github.com/kpu/kenlm); [PyLaia docs](https://doc.teklia.com/pylaia/usage/language_models/)); PyLaia 1.1.2 (MIT) ships this decoding natively if the project prefers a second engine to patching kraken ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/pylaia/)). Lexicon-free character LMs suit abbreviated, variably spelled text, where a lexicon would force out names and abbreviations. Decoding is CPU-bound, 0.12 s per line image against 0.03 s for greedy decoding on CPU, so 300,000 lines cost about 10 single-threaded CPU-hours and parallelise trivially ([arXiv 2404.18722](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18722)). The decoder carries over unchanged to PP-OCRv6, which is also a CTC model, and the notes found no public project that already wires kraken to KenLM, so this would be a reusable contribution.

Two pitfalls can turn the gain negative. **Leakage**: an LM trained on the full Academy edition contains the text of every edited test document, so the edition text of all evaluation documents must stay out of the LM corpus. **Convention mismatch**: the edition expands abbreviations and normalises u/v, i/j and long s, while the ground truth and codec may not; unless the LM corpus is mapped to the codec's conventions and Unicode form (NFD for the FoNDUE family), the LM pushes output toward edition spelling. Normalisation choices can reverse conclusions outright: GPT-4o's correction improved Finnish OCR by 11.9 % under long-s and w/v normalisation and worsened it by 10.1 % without ([Kanerva et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01205v1)). For documents the edition already covers, aligning its text to the image beats recognising it; the LM earns its keep on unedited material.

An in-domain post-corrector is the next lever, with a higher ceiling and more variance. mT5-small (about 300 M parameters) trained on a recogniser's own error profile cut READ 2016 German line CER from **2.71 % to 1.65 %** ([HAND](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.18981)); ByT5-small on a Medieval Greek manuscript cut CER from 12.54 % to 11.55 % on automatic lines but had "smaller and less consistent" effects, including CER degradation, on corrected lines ([KONVENS 2026](https://www.korpuslab.uni-hamburg.de/en/konvens2026/121.pdf)); and screening lines with a classifier before correction reduced the corrector's own mistakes ([Pavlopoulos et al.](https://eprints.cs.univie.ac.at/8012)). Training pairs come cheaply from running cross-validated kraken models over the aligned lines. The notes estimate **10–40 % relative at about 8 % CER**, shrinking on clean lines, an estimate transferred from settings unlike Leibniz's. Over-correction of low-noise input is the documented failure mode even for the winner of ICDAR 2026's HIPE-OCRepair, which cut printed-text error from 2.26 % to 0.50 % with a continued-pretrained 24 B model far beyond one rented card ([arXiv 2607.08143](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08143)); gating the corrector to low-confidence lines is the standard defence. Zero-shot, text-only LLM correction is not an option: 14 LLMs "mostly degrade the input text" on historical benchmarks that include German-Latin commentaries ([Boros et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2024.latechclfl-1.14/)), and every open model tested raised CER on Finnish by 19–77 % ([Kanerva et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01205v1)).

Ensembling is plausible but unmeasured in the form the project would use. Five-model n-best voting cut a TrOCR's CER on Gwalther's Latin letters from 1.86 % to 1.60 % (−14 %), with the top five apparently chosen on the same validation set ([arXiv 2508.11499](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11499)), and older printed-book work cut errors by "up to 50 % and more" with cross-fold voting ([Reul et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1711.09670)), but no 2022–2026 study measured ROVER-style combination of a CTC model with an encoder–decoder model on historical handwriting. An oracle choosing the best system per page reached 89.4 against 83.1 for the best single model on CHURRO's German handwriting ([arXiv 2509.19768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19768)), so headroom exists for per-line selection by confidence among FoNDUE-GD-, PP-OCRv6- and party-based models, at N times the training cost and only after each component is tuned.

## Vision-language models belong in the review loop, not the reading seat

The project's B1 test is the most relevant measurement: on 150 pre-segmented Leibniz Latin/French lines, zero-shot gpt-4.1 scored **36.6 % CER**, gpt-4o 43.8 %, gemini-3.8-flash 55.1 % and the GPT-5.6 models 74–79 %, against 8.19 % for the fine-tuned kraken model on the same lines, and the larger models most often modernised archaic spelling ([B1 report](https://zenodo.org/records/22782817)). The panel omitted Claude and Gemini Pro-tier models, which independent benchmarks rank first on handwriting: in Teklia's METATR, Gemini 3 Pro scored 28.5 % and Claude Opus 4.5 31.2 % CER across handwritten documents against 59.5 % for GPT-5.1, and on French manuscripts of 1600–1800 Gemini 3 Pro reached 10.2 % and Claude 13.0 %, though on ten-sample sets ([METATR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26712)). On German the same two models still scored 48.8 % (Claude) and 56.3 % (Gemini) on READ 2016 pages, and the one Kurrent-specific study, at the Goethe- und Schiller-Archiv, found that multimodal LLMs alone gave "deutlich schlechtere" (clearly worse) recognition than Transkribus, with "stark halluzinierte Ergebnisse" (strongly hallucinated results) on letters ([DHd 2026](https://zenodo.org/records/18696394)). The SPECS claim that frontier LLMs reach "70-80% CER zero-shot" on Kurrent is a 2024-vintage number that still fits GPT-5-class and open 3–9 B models but not Gemini 3- or Claude Opus-class ones ([SPECS](https://github.com/marchofhares/leibnizlegible/blob/main/SPECS.md); [Crosilla et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.15195)). The decision not to use VLMs as primary readers stands all the same: even a model that halved B1's best result would sit near 18 %, more than twice the kraken model's error (hypothetical arithmetic).

Fine-tuned VLMs win only at scale, and not reliably against a tuned CTC model. CHURRO's Qwen2.5-VL-3B, fully fine-tuned on 99,491 pages with roughly 800 H100-hours, lifted German handwriting similarity from 51.2 to 83.1, but its weights fall under the non-commercial Qwen Research License ([arXiv 2509.19768](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19768); [Qwen licence](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct/blob/main/LICENSE)). The École des chartes' MEDUSA line recognisers (Qwen3.5-4B and 9B, CC BY 4.0 weights, about 645,000 training lines) claimed competition results that kraken's author could not reproduce; in his runs the 9B model lost to PP-OCRv6 medium on Latin, 13.07 % against 8.70 %, and ran about 45 times slower ([HF Medusa](https://huggingface.co/ENC-PSL/Medusa0.1Line-4B); [arXiv 2609.20064](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20064)). A LoRA run fits the 24 GB card, Qwen3-VL-4B comfortably in bf16 and 7–9 B models with 4-bit QLoRA following recipes such as Ludwig's ([Ludwig](https://ludwig.ai/latest/examples/vlm_finetuning/)), but no published head-to-head on a few thousand historical lines shows a VLM beating kraken or TrOCR, so it is a research bet, not an expected gain.

The evidence supports a different job: a second reader from another model family whose disagreements route human attention. When frontier models corrected drafts of English handwriting using the page image, strict CER fell from 8.0 % to 3.0 % for a Transkribus draft (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) and from 10.3 % to 4.0 % for a PyLaia draft; correction by a different family improved CER by 15–20 %, self-correction by about 1 % ([Humphries et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.03340)). On medieval Latin legal hands, Gemini 3 Pro correcting a CNN-LSTM-plus-KenLM draft from the text alone lowered CER from **6.0 % to 3.8 %** and improved 19 of 20 cases, yet three of its four remaining errors in one excerpt were "misleading", fluent and wrong, where the raw model's errors were visibly odd ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00977)). Disagreement flagging between model families caught about 76 % of residual errors while sending about 4 % of the text to a human ([Generative History](https://generativehistory.substack.com/p/when-models-disagree-transcription)), and an informal replication found that an automated resolver did not beat the primary reader, so adjudication needs a person ([GitHub issue](https://github.com/Embassy-of-the-Free-Mind/sourcelibrary-v2/issues/6547)). For Kurrent, the Weimar team's workable pattern was an image-only MLLM transcription first, then a prompt merging it with the HTR text, then human review in a diff view; single prompts asking a model to correct HTR text while preserving historical spelling failed through "instruction conflict", and the merge helped clearly only on the more legible poetry manuscripts ([DHd 2026](https://zenodo.org/records/18696394)). For the Latin/French track, at around 8 % CER and inside the 3–10 % band where correction has helped, the defensible design routes low-confidence lines to Gemini 3.x Pro or Claude Sonnet/Opus 5.x with image and draft, runs each twice, flags disagreements, rejects outputs outside a length-ratio guard such as the HIPE teams' 0.35–2.8× ([arXiv 2607.08143](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08143)), and stores accepted edits as `status='corrected'` with the model version, as the project's SPECS already plan. For Kurrent at 30–50 %, no study shows correction rescuing a draft, so VLMs there serve only as flaggers.

Cost is not the constraint; terms are. At list prices of 26 October 2026, the notes estimate roughly $7.5–15 per 1,000 pages for Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (batch or standard, no reasoning), $9–19 for Claude Sonnet 5.5 and $19–37 for Claude Opus 5.5, rising about four- to fivefold if reasoning tokens run uncapped; the one measured anchor is about $9–10 per 1,000 English pages for Gemini 3 Pro at minimal thinking ([Gemini pricing](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing); [Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing); [Generative History](https://generativehistory.substack.com/p/gemini-3-solves-handwriting-recognition)). Rerunning B1's 150 lines with these models, plus 150 honest-test Kurrent lines, costs a few dollars (an estimate; B1's runs cost $0.03–4.98 per model). The binding issues are contractual. Anthropic's Commercial Terms forbid using the services "to train competing AI models" and Google's Gemini terms forbid developing "models that compete with the Services", so API outputs should feed review, never the training set of a released model ([Anthropic terms](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms); [Gemini terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms)). Paid tiers do not train on inputs by default but do retain them: Anthropic's Batch API, the 50 % discount route the SPECS plan, keeps jobs for 29 days and is not eligible for zero retention ([data retention](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/api-and-data-retention)), so the project needs an explicit rule on whether its NC-bucket text may leave the house. Self-hosted open VLMs avoid both problems when their licence is clean: Qwen3-VL, Qwen3.5, Qwen2.5-VL-7B/32B, Gemma 4 and Pixtral-12B are Apache-2.0, whereas Qwen2.5-VL-3B and its CHURRO derivative are non-commercial, Gemma 3's terms reach models trained on its synthetic outputs, Llama 3.2/4 multimodal rights are not granted to EU-domiciled licensees and Nougat's weights are CC BY-NC ([Qwen3-VL-8B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct); [Gemma 4](https://huggingface.co/google/gemma-4-12B-it); [Gemma terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/terms); [Llama 3.2 policy](https://github.com/meta-llama/llama-models/blob/main/models/llama3_2/USE_POLICY.md)). Model retirements also mean API transcriptions cannot be regenerated later, which is a further reason to record model versions.

## Licence traps: NC relicensing, ShareAlike inputs and unfounded MIT tags

Creative Commons' May 2025 guidance gives the conservative frame. CC terms apply only where copyright permission is needed; **NonCommercial reaches training itself**, while BY, SA and ND conditions trigger only on public sharing; a publicly shared model built on ShareAlike data "should be made available under the same CC license"; and following the guidance "will almost certainly lead to overcompliance" ([CC guide](https://creativecommons.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Using-CC-licensed-Works-for-AI-Training.pdf)). Field practice is looser: UB Mannheim's CC BY-SA model was trained on the NC-SA Basel set, and the Apache-2.0 party and PP-OCRv6 bases list NC-SA, SA and restricted datasets among their sources ([Zenodo 7933463](https://zenodo.org/records/7933463); [Zenodo 20642057](https://zenodo.org/records/20642057); [Zenodo 21788405](https://zenodo.org/records/21788405)). That shows what others do, not what the law requires; how SA and NC propagate to model weights is unsettled, and nothing here is legal advice. Two non-licence pitfalls travel with these resources. Evaluation contamination: party trained on Dresden K.113 and the Leopold set, and CHURRO's corpus includes the Dresden 1665 diary and READ Bozen, so Dresden- or Bozen-based comparisons flatter those models; METATR's 15.4 % for Churro-3B on READ 2016 is an in-domain score ([METATR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26712)), and the GWLB honest set remains the only fair German yardstick. Catalogue metadata also misleads: HTR-United and Zenodo disagree on Basel's licence, the Dresden records disagree with themselves, and FoNDUE READMEs show CC BY 2.0 logos beside 4.0 catalogue entries ([FONDUE-FR-MSS-18 README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/FoNDUE-HTR/FONDUE-FR-MSS-18/main/README.md)), so every licence should be read at the deposit and archived with the data.

## Conclusion

The remaining gains on both tracks sit in assets only the project can create. No download supplies German in Leibniz's hand, but a few hundred verified own-hand lines and the CC BY aligned lines would together form the first open German letter ground truth of the 1690–1716 decades, a gap every 2024–2026 release leaves open and one the project could fill by publishing them with an `htr-united.yml`, as the Leopold and FoNDUE deposits do. The licence shock on the Latin/French side points the same way: the rebuild it forces is also the project's first chance to own its base model, its test set and its licence terms outright, instead of inheriting all three from PHILIUMM.

The second implication concerns architecture. Calibrated line confidence is the hinge for nearly every lever after the base model: a beam decoder with an LM produces per-line scores, and those scores decide which lines a ByT5 corrector may touch, which go to a VLM second reader and then a human, and which unlabelled lines are safe pseudo-labels for self-training. Building the decoding-and-confidence layer early multiplies the value of each later step, while VLMs, fluent, costly to audit and contractually barred from feeding released models, are best kept as dissenting readers that show humans where to look. In both queues below, costs assume roughly $0.3–0.5 per hour for a rented 24 GB card, an assumption rather than a quoted price, and every gain not tied to a cited measurement is an estimate.
